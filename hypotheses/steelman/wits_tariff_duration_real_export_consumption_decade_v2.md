# Steelman: tariff-duration real-export and consumption calibration

The plausible mechanism is persistent protection of domestic producers. Long
periods of tariffs above a material threshold can reduce competitive pressure,
slow upgrading, and raise the cost of imported inputs or household goods.
If that mechanism dominates, countries with more protected years in 2001–2010
should have weaker real export and real private-consumption-per-person growth
in 2011–2020, even after matching the earlier trend in each outcome.

The strongest competing account is selection: economies that struggled to
export or raise incomes might have kept tariffs high. The weighted tariff also
changes with import composition without a statute changing. Commodity prices,
exchange rates, financial crises, and COVID-19 can affect the following
decade. Current tariff level, initial income, rule of law, terms of trade,
investment, and earlier outcome growth are prespecified covariates, but they
do not turn this cross-country association into a causal effect.

The two outcomes are deliberately joint. A tariff-duration association with
exports alone does not demonstrate a household-consumption drag. A positive
association in either endpoint at the frozen magnitude and error threshold
refutes the stated two-outcome pattern. Missing annual tariff values, weak
exposure variation, or an unstable fit stays inconclusive. The result never
grades the archived original ID or grants policy, school, position, causal,
or featured credit.
