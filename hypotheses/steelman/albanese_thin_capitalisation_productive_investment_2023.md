# Strongest opposing account: thin capitalisation and productive investment

The strongest opposing argument is that the reform primarily changes financing and profit allocation, rather than the amount of productive Australian capital. A multinational can finance the same factory or service operation with equity, external debt or a different group capital structure. If part of its prior related-party interest merely shifted taxable profit abroad, limiting that deduction can increase Australian revenue without reducing the investment that generated the income. It may also improve competitive neutrality for domestic businesses that lacked comparable tax-planning opportunities.

The [enacted 2024 law](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2024A00023/asmade/2024-04-08/text/original/epub/OEBPS/document_1/document_1.html) does not apply one identical 30% constraint to every business. Exemptions, alternative tests, tax-EBITDA definitions and carried-forward deductions change the effective burden. An analysis that labels all multinationals treated, uses accounting EBITDA in place of statutory tax EBITDA, or assumes the default test always binds will misclassify both treatment and tax cost. The [Treasury tax-expenditure statement](https://treasury.gov.au/sites/default/files/2025-12/p2025-721342.pdf) describes several distinctions that must be resolved at group level.

Newly constrained firms are likely to be highly leveraged or to have unusually low taxable earnings. Those traits also predict sensitivity to higher interest rates, demand weakness and the end of pandemic investment incentives. A post-2023 fall in their investment, even with firm fixed effects, may reflect those financing and demand shocks. Retrospective legal application does not erase anticipation: the policy was discussed before enactment, and firms could have adjusted before the nominal treatment year.

Investment measurement matters equally. Lower tax depreciation deductions do not demonstrate lower capital purchases. Moving an asset between subsidiaries can change reported entities without changing national capital. A treated group may sell a viable project to an exempt or better-capitalised Australian owner. These possibilities require real productive-acquisition measures, successor linkage and investment by other firms, not simply the largest available drop in a tax-return field.

The alternative would gain support if adequately powered independent counterfactual designs exclude a decline of 1 percentage point in the prespecified investment rate, and would gain stronger support if both 90% intervals lie within the proposed -0.5 to +0.5 point equivalence band. A simultaneous shift from related-party debt to equity, greater Australian taxable profits and stable productive investment would fit the mechanism especially well. A failure to obtain confidential tax fields or a wide confidence interval is not supporting evidence for the alternative.

There is nevertheless a serious adverse case: some groups cannot readily replace debt, and tax losses or carryforward restrictions may make investment unusually sensitive to deduction limits. A well-identified effect on those firms should not be dismissed because avoidance reduction is a legitimate objective. Its magnitude and broader importance still depend on domestic reallocation, wages, ownership incidence and the value of public receipts. The [Board of Taxation review](https://taxboard.gov.au/review/thin-capitalisation-reforms) is an evidence-development route, not a completed causal verdict. This document is the substantive opposing account for an untested candidate, not a registration or estimate.
