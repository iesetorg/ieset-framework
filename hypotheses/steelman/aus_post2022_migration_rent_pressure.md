# Strongest alternative explanation

The housing shortage predates the Albanese government and cannot be attributed to a government by placing its election date on a rent chart. Household sizes fell during the pandemic, construction became more expensive, financing tightened, and planning and infrastructure constrained supply. Rent growth before borders reopened is especially important evidence against a simple migration-only explanation. The RBA's July 2025 analysis concludes that increased international student numbers probably explain only a small share of the rent rise since the pandemic began. That finding concerns students and the full pandemic window; it does not prove zero effect for all migration or every neighbourhood.

People often settle where jobs, universities and existing communities are concentrated. Those places can have rising rents for the same reasons that attract arrivals. Internal moves also redistribute demand beyond initial settlement locations. Even a positive regional migration coefficient after controls can therefore reflect selection, common demand shocks or incomplete measurement. Conversely, migration can support housing supply through construction workers and engineers, and indirectly through a stronger market for investment. The proposed annual lag does not make the flow exogenous or isolate a federal policy.

The material threshold of 0.5 percentage points of additional annual real rent growth per one percentage point of NOM share is a proposed decision rule, not a known structural elasticity. If a well-measured estimate rules out an effect of that size, the candidate's material claim should be falsified even when its estimate is weakly positive. If the confidence interval is wide, the honest result is inconclusive. If the rent association appears only in the preferred model, disappears when influential markets are removed, or reverses with reasonable geography/vintage corrections, it should remain mixed or inconclusive.

New-lease rents capture the market facing people who move, while incumbent tenants may experience a different path. Quality deterioration, crowding or reduced household formation can conceal stress in an apparently moderate price index. Conversely, a rent rise is partly a transfer to property owners, so it cannot be counted in full as national economic destruction. The distributional burden on low-income renters can still be serious and must be measured alongside income changes, new housing and search costs.

The competing explanation would gain strength if post-2022 rent increases were largely explained by pre-existing supply scarcity, household formation and financing exposure, if the specified migration association were precisely below its threshold, or if migrant construction labour generated offsetting supply. Strong support for the candidate requires the opposite pattern across credible regional data and sensitivity checks. Even then, it would support an association in a defined sample, not a verdict on the government's immigration policy or overall welfare.
