# Steelman: france_reference_rents_paris_lille_phase_in

## Counter-argument

The Paris annulment (2017-2019) was a legal-administrative event, not a
natural experiment: enforcement had already weakened before annulment, and
landlords priced the reinstatement probability (explicitly promised by the
2018 ELAN framework) into new leases during the off years, so the absence
of an effect in the annulment window may reflect anticipated re-imposition
rather than controls that never bound. Lille's 2020 start coincides with
the pandemic rent correction, contaminating its first two treated years.

## Strongest empirical objections

1. **Annulment window contamination**: 2017-2019 Paris overlaps the
   Yellow-Vest-era demand shock and the 2018 ELAN reforms of the rental
   sector, either of which could mask or mimic an on/off contrast.

2. **Supplement leakage**: the complément de loyer mechanism lets landlords
   charge above the reference with documentation, so measured new-lease
   rent effects may be absorbed into supplement shares rather than levels.

3. **Coverage selectivity**: OLL panels cover larger professional
   landlords disproportionately; small landlords — the margin where exit
   is likeliest — are underrepresented, biasing composition-shift
   estimates toward zero.

## What would change our mind

If supplement shares absorb the entire active-year rent gap (levels flat,
supplements up), the cap is formally binding but economically porous; if
the annulment window shows no rent catch-up while supplements stay flat,
the "controls bind" premise weakens and the French case should be
re-classified as incidence-only evidence.
