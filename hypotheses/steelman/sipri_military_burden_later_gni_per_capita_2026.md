# Steelman: military burden and later real GNI per person

A greater fraction of national output devoted to the military could coincide with fewer resources for other development priorities, so the frozen directional prediction is a smaller later improvement. This is an opportunity-cost mechanism, not an identified spending substitution.

The strongest alternative is that security threats or wars raise military burden while independently harming health, schooling and income. Recessions can raise the military share by shrinking GDP even without higher spending. Reverse prioritization and slow-moving development trends also matter. A negative association cannot establish that military spending caused any change; a null or positive estimate weakens only this predictive pattern.
