# Steelman: OECD tertiary migrant/native employment gap × EPL

A stricter regular-worker employment-protection rule could reduce new hiring
and make employers more cautious with workers whose foreign credentials or
local experience they know less well. Even tertiary-educated foreign-born
workers could then lose employment access relative to native-born peers.
A within-country annual increase in the OECD regular-worker EPL index
followed by a wider native-minus-foreign employment-rate gap would fit that
mechanism.

The reverse is plausible. Protection can stabilize existing foreign-born
jobs, and migrant hiring may concentrate in sectors or contract types that
this regular-worker index does not describe. Reform may arrive precisely
when a government sees weak employment, making the observed timing a response
to conditions. A birth-place employment-rate gap also mixes immigration
cohorts, ages, education recognition, and sector composition. Pandemic-era
shocks can vary sharply across these groups even when a common year effect
is included. The predeclared model cannot separate these channels and is
not a causal test of immigration or employment-protection policy.

An observed null, reverse sign, sample-floor failure, or sensitivity to the
pandemic diagnostic should remain visible. The outcome is an OECD employment
rate, not migrant inflow or tertiary-attainment share; those substitutions
would change the question.
