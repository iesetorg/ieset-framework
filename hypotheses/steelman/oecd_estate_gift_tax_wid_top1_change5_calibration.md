# Steelman: estate and gift-tax receipts versus later wealth concentration

The strongest competing account is reverse causality. An economy with more
large estates may collect more category 4300 tax without a stronger tax law;
asset-price gains can raise both receipts and the top 1% share. The receipt
ratio also moves with deaths, exemptions, gifts, tax-base valuation, total
tax receipts, refunds and enforcement. It is not a statutory rate, a tax-base
coverage score, or evidence of a reform. Country and tax-year fixed effects
do not remove time-varying country factors. A five-year lag does not make the
observed association causal.

WID net-wealth shares combine surveys, tax records, national accounts and
distributional estimates. Some country-year values can be modeled or
interpolated, and method changes can alter a measured five-year change.
Because net wealth can be negative for other groups, a top-group share above
100% is mathematically possible, though such values warrant attention.
The exact source is retained with no value-based trimming; an influential
country can be detected by the registered leave-one-country-out and exposure
concentration checks. Those checks cannot repair noncomparability or
confounding.

A weak, zero, or positive coefficient is credible: wealth concentration may
expand the taxable bequest base, avoidance can erode collections, and other
tax and transfer rules may dominate. The test reports the outcome under its
frozen rule even if it conflicts with the negative prior. A policy-effect
claim would require a separate timed reform design with law, enforcement,
migration, valuation and pretrend evidence. This study is only a descriptive
calibration and is barred from causal, policy, school, and featured credit.
