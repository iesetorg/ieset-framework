# Remittance change and later secondary gross enrolment: rival explanations

Household transfers from abroad can finance fees, transport, supplies, and
time away from paid work. A rising remittance share could therefore precede
higher secondary enrolment. The fixed t-to-t+2 comparison gives families
time to respond and uses the t ratio as a baseline.

The strongest rival explanation is recipient and migrant selection. Families
with relatives abroad may already value education, be richer, or live in
regions with expanding schools. Destination-country wage cycles can move
remittances while origin-country reforms move enrolment. A lower origin GDP
denominator can raise remittances as a share even when transfer amounts do
not rise. Country and year effects and two GDP controls cannot remove these
time-varying differences.

The outcome is a gross ratio. Over-age pupils, repetition, a revised
school-age population denominator, or changes in education reporting can
raise it without additional on-time attendance, completion, or learning.
Transfers may instead fund primary school, tertiary study, private
tutoring, or consumption. A null or adverse result under this frozen window
does not refute every schooling channel.

The test must retain all countries and valid large exposure changes in the
fixed roster, both missing and measured cases, and any adverse verdict.
It earns descriptive calibration only, with no causal, policy, school, or
featured credit.
