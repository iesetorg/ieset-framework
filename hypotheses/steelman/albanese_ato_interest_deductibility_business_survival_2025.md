# Strongest opposing account: ATO-interest deductibility and business survival

The strongest alternative is that repayment enforcement exposed accumulated financial weakness, while removing an interest deduction restored fair treatment between businesses that paid tax on time and those using the ATO as a lender. A firm already making losses may have been unable to use the deduction immediately. For that firm, the legal change need not create the cash shock suggested by multiplying its entire interest bill by the statutory company tax rate. Repayment, remission, refinancing or renewed tax compliance can also absorb the change without destroying a viable operation.

The causal question would require comparing the same collection regime with the deduction retained. The current screen does not identify that counterfactual; it compares high- and low-exposure firms that survived to implementation. The ANAO's [30 June 2026 audit](https://www.anao.gov.au/work/performance-audit/australian-taxation-office-management-of-small-business-taxpayer-debt) documents debt accumulated over several years and firmer recovery actions resuming in 2023-24. Those actions, interest-rate exposure and weak sector demand can produce insolvencies even if the deduction reform has little independent effect. Tax arrears are also a signal of prior distress: comparing tax-debt firms with compliant businesses without matching their trajectories would build the desired conclusion into the sample.

Formal external administration is not equivalent to a destroyed business. A restructuring may preserve trading, employees and assets; a sale may transfer productive activity. Conversely, a surviving legal entity can shed jobs and stop investing. The proposed primary outcome consequently needs payroll, continuation and successor evidence, while preserving the original outcome even if a secondary measure looks more favourable.

The latest [ASIC annual context](https://www.asic.gov.au/about-asic/corporate-publications/newsletters/asic-corporate-insolvency-update/asic-corporate-insolvency-update-issue-41-october-2026) reports fewer first administrations in 2025-26 than in 2024-25. That aggregate improvement cannot disprove harm to highly exposed firms, just as earlier aggregate increases cannot establish harm from this particular law. It does make an automatic narrative of accelerating economy-wide destruction untenable without a properly scoped test.

Evidence against the specified material vulnerability association would be a valid, adequately powered pair of adjusted comparisons placing the entire 95% interval below the proposed +0.5 percentage-point threshold. Practical equivalence would require both narrower 90% intervals to fit within -0.2 to +0.2 points. Stable employment and investment alongside faster repayment would strengthen the alternative mechanism. Imprecise estimates or invalid control groups would establish neither view.

A harmful causal survival effect remains possible but is not identified here, particularly for viable firms with an immediately usable deduction and limited refinancing options. If it is measured, it must remain visible even if revenue or competitive-neutrality gains are also found. Equally, those gains cannot be ignored when moving from a narrow business-survival claim to a judgment about welfare. This steelman is an untested opposing account accompanying a candidate import draft; it is not evidence of a completed review or a preregistered result.

Selecting firms that survived until 1 July 2025 forces their earlier first-administration outcomes to zero. An event study or synthetic control fitted to those zero histories would produce misleading reassurance. Distinct historical at-risk cohorts, transport assumptions and anticipation-selection bounds are necessary for a future causal protocol. Both high- and low-exposure firms can be affected by the law, so even their adjusted difference cannot equal the absolute policy effect automatically.
