# Strongest argument against the proposed benefit

Longer permission to stay does not guarantee more effective skilled labour supply. A graduate may have remained on another visa, worked below their qualification, lacked occupational licensing, or competed with an incumbent worker for an existing position. More eligible graduates resident in Australia could therefore coexist with little change in skill shortages, productivity or total employment. A policy selected using shortage lists also risks chasing a temporary problem after labour demand has changed.

The federal government introduced the extension on 1 July 2023 for specified qualifications and later withdrew access in mid-2024. That reconsideration is a reason to investigate the policy, not itself evidence that it failed. Employers might have needed faster visa processing, recognition of credentials or a more stable permanent pathway instead of additional temporary years. Administrative uncertainty and employer sponsorship preferences could prevent graduates from converting legal permission into skilled work. The relevant question is what happened after the graduates' original permissions would have ended, not whether the visa stock increased immediately after the announcement.

The central identification problem is that the eligibility list was chosen because of perceived shortages. Eligible and ineligible fields were consequently different before treatment, and their prospects might have diverged even without the extension. The post-pandemic labour-market recovery, changes in student composition and other visa reforms further complicate comparisons with 2015-2017. Provider and field controls, reweighting and event-study diagnostics reduce some concerns but do not prove an exogenous policy experiment. This candidate therefore carries an associational evidence label. If credible overlap or comparable trends cannot be established, it should remain inconclusive rather than become a causal finding by assertion.

A joint outcome requiring both residence and employment in an eligible shortage occupation makes the test stronger than counting visa grants. Nevertheless, the chosen five-percentage-point threshold is a proposed policy-relevance rule, not a forecast backed by results. A precise interval below that threshold falsifies the stated material-benefit claim; a noisy null does not. Missing occupation information must not be counted as unsuccessful employment, and incomplete 24-month follow-up must not be quietly replaced by an earlier endpoint. Occupational coding should describe the person's job, not simply the degree or employer's industry.

The strongest case for the policy is that Australia had already educated these graduates, and extending their rights could retain complementary skills while employers filled vacancies and households received needed services. Earlier Australian evidence reviewed by the Productivity Commission does not support a blanket presumption that immigration depresses wages, although particular labour markets can experience losses. That benefit deserves a serious test alongside housing demand, displacement, exploitation and fiscal effects. A material, robust increase in matched skilled employment would support the limited retention proposition, while net additional labour supply and net welfare would still require the separately declared second-order measurements.
