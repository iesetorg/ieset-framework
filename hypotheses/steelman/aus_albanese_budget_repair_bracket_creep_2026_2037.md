# Strongest opposing case

Budget forecasts normally assume current policy rather than speculative future tax cuts. A forecast in which average tax rates increase does not prove an intention to impose every projected increase. Future governments can return revenue through rate changes, offsets or threshold changes, or substitute other taxes. This projection is conditional on its stated vintage.

Non-indexation predates the Albanese government. Tax relief already announced must be included in both scenarios, and growth in income-tax receipts also reflects employment, real incomes, the income distribution and explicit tax-base changes. It is not valid to label the entire tax-revenue increase as bracket creep.

The competing explanation is that forecast expenditure restraint and other revenue measures deliver most primary-balance repair. A reproducible indexation scenario could show that threshold non-indexation accounts for half or less, rejecting the claim at its specified magnitude. CPI and wage indexation answer different questions, so results must not be interchanged.

Even a supported dependence result is not a welfare finding: reduced revenue requires some combination of different taxes, spending, borrowing or service provision. Behavioural and distributional effects are separate empirical questions. Known public discussion of bracket creep motivated this candidate; it is not being represented as a novel prediction made before seeing all relevant commentary.
