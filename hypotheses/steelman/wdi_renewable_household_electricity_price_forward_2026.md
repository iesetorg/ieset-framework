# Renewable electricity share and later household price: strongest case and limits

An increase in renewable generation share can change system costs that eventually reach households: new generation, grid reinforcement, backup capacity, retail contracts, and policy levies may all matter. A direct [Eurostat household-price series](https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/cache/metadata/EN/nrg_pc_204_sims_ee.htm) tests a price outcome rather than the CO2 proxy in the older alias candidate. [World Bank metadata](https://databank.worldbank.org/metadataglossary/world-development-indicators/series/EG.ELC.RNEW.ZS) defines the exposure as renewable electricity generated divided by total generated electricity, with IEA as the source.

The main objection is confounding. A renewable share can rise because hydro generation changes, fossil output falls, or total demand shifts. Gas and import prices, exchange-rate conversion into euro, taxes, household subsidies, network charges, and retail price controls can move the household bill without a renewable buildout. The selected 2,500–4,999 kWh band is a medium household group, not industry or a national mean across all consumers. A one-year lag does not isolate grid-policy effects. The 2018+ crisis period may dominate, so the frozen pre-2018 check must independently clear directional support or refutation.

Country and year effects, a full-rank requirement, exposure concentration caps, minimum country-year/material-change floors, and a committed source-lock history make the descriptive association inspectable. Neither sign can prove a renewable mandate raised or lowered prices; no causal, policy, school-score, position, or featured credit is assigned. Source metadata and redistribution rights require separate review before any public raw-file release.
