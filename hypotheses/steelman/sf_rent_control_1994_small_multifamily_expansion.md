# Steelman: sf_rent_control_1994_small_multifamily_expansion

## Counter-argument

The 1994 expansion affected a small, old building class already partially
covered by eviction protections, in a housing market where land values and
condo-conversion incentives were driven by the 1990s tech boom rather than
by rent-control status. Conversion and maintenance differences between
2-4 unit pre-1980 buildings and exempt stock may reflect owner-occupancy
demand and Proposition 13 tax lock-in, not the regulatory change. SF's
long-run rental-supply decline predates 1994 and is visible in exempt
classes too.

## Strongest empirical objections

1. **Siting on a boom**: 1995-2005 San Francisco conversion waves track the
   dot-com and early-2000s price cycles; donor cities (Oakland, San Jose)
   shared the cycle without sharing the treatment, but their building-class
   composition differs.

2. **Classification leakage**: Assessor building-class reclassifications
   after conversion mechanically reduce "rental" counts for treated classes
   even when units stay rented.

3. **Incumbent discount overstated**: rent discounts for long-tenured
   incumbents partly reflect tenure length itself (national pattern) rather
   than control status; entrant-vs-incumbent gaps exist in uncontrolled
   markets too.

## What would change our mind

Event-study pre-trends in the treated class diverging from exempt stock
before 1994 would attribute the post-period gap to pre-existing trends;
a null with clean pre-trends in both legs would move the axis prior toward
"small-building expansions are too marginal to move supply."
