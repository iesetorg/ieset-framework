# Steelman: geographic holdout of business entry and rule of law

The strongest alternative is that business-registration counts measure formal
paperwork, not new productive firms. Digital filing, tax incentives, legal
changes, and shell-company formation can increase registrations without
creating productive capacity. Such administrative changes may also be more
common in countries with stronger rule of law, producing a positive interaction
even if legal institutions do not amplify the growth value of genuine entry.

Rule of law may proxy for income, state capacity, education, access to finance,
or the ability to record registrations. Country and year fixed effects remove
stable country differences and common year shocks, but they do not remove
time-varying omitted factors. Current-year growth is an incomplete control for
business-cycle dynamics. Formal entry and later growth may also respond to
the same expectations, so reverse causality remains possible.

The geographic holdout is a meaningful check on whether the old association
was confined to its selected 60 countries. It is not a causal replication:
the held-out countries may have less comparable registers and a different
income distribution. A weak, negative, or imprecise interaction should remain
visible rather than being explained away by selecting a more favorable subset.
