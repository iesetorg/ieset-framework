# Steelman: recurrent wealth-tax receipts and top-1 wealth share

The strongest objection is that tax *receipts* are jointly determined with
wealth concentration. A country can collect more because its wealthy tax base
is larger, because its rules cover more assets, because its administration is
stronger, or because GDP fell. A negative or positive panel coefficient cannot
isolate statutory tax policy. The three-year offset blocks exact simultaneity
but does not cure policy anticipation, persistent trends, migration, valuation,
or reverse causality.

WID wealth shares are estimated and sometimes interpolated. Method changes or
survey and capitalization differences may move the measured share without a
real distributional change. The OECD series covers recurrent taxes on net
wealth, not the full tax-and-transfer system. Missingness may favor countries
with stronger statistical capacity. Country and tax-year effects remove stable
country differences and common year shocks, but country-specific trends and
other policies remain. A leave-one-country-out sign check can expose dominance
by one country; it cannot solve these identification problems.

Even a precisely negative coefficient would only be a descriptive association
for this pinned panel. An opposing account predicts a weak, zero, or positive
association because more concentrated wealth enlarges the taxable base, or
because avoidance and asset relocation weaken recorded receipts. A defensible
policy claim needs a separate, well-timed reform design with statutory-rate,
base, enforcement, migration, and valuation evidence. This test is deliberately
ineligible for that credit.
