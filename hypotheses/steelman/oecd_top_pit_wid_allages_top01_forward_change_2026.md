# Top PIT rate and WID all-ages top-0.1% share: steelman

The strongest reason to expect a negative forward association is that a higher top statutory rate can compress the income recorded at the very top through changes in bargaining, compensation form, timing, and reporting. The test deliberately uses the WID all-ages `sptincj999` pre-tax national-income share and a next-year change, so it asks a narrower question than whether a tax reform reduced disposable-income inequality.

The strongest objection is measurement and selection. The OECD rate describes the highest gross-wage tax bracket, whereas WID top incomes include labour and capital income. Tax bases, credits, enforcement, capital-gains treatment, and local tax details differ. WID estimates can use fiscal records, so reporting changes may move the measured share without an equivalent change in underlying resources. Governments may change top rates in response to inequality or shocks; country and year effects do not remove those time-varying confounders. The historical series has already been seen in related studies, and the outcome is not an independent holdout.

The pre-registered test therefore gives only descriptive calibration credit. The tax-policy scope tag identifies the measured exposure; it creates no policy endorsement or score. A significant association in either direction is evidence about this exact measured relationship, not a causal tax-policy effect. A weak or unidentified fit remains partial or inconclusive under the frozen rules. The inference uses country-clustered uncertainty and a two-sided Student-t reference with G−1 country-cluster degrees of freedom. School, causal, and policy scores stay at zero.
